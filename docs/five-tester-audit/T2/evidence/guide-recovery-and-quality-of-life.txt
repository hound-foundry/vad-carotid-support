Recovery, fatigue and quality of life
Make room for changes in energy, confidence, mood and everyday life, even when they are hard to see. About 2 min read.

There is room for what is hard
Recovery can touch energy, pain, sleep, concentration, confidence, mood, relationships, work and social life. Some changes are easy for other people to see; others are private or come and go. You do not have to prove that a concern is serious before raising it with a clinician.
A metaphor some people find helpful
Some people use spoon theory as a way to describe how everyday tasks can draw on a limited amount of energy, and why choices may be needed when fatigue is present. The metaphor comes from Christine Miserandino’s account of her own experience living with lupus; it is not a clinical measure, does not establish what fatigue is like after a dissection or stroke, and may not fit everyone.

A small and limited research signal
A long-term study of cervical artery dissection survivors reported that quality of life may not be captured fully by standard disability ratings. A newer exploratory study followed a selected group with good early functional outcomes and still found long-term fatigue and psychological symptoms in some participants. These studies cannot predict an individual’s experience, establish a general prevalence, or show that this app changes outcomes.
Support for people around you
Family and friends may notice changes or have questions of their own. If you want, choose what to share and ask someone to help with a specific task. The person supporting you can also ask their own care team or local support service about help for carers.
